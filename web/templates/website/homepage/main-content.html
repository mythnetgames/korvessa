One hundred and sixty years ago, divine cataclysms drove humanity into five sacred cities. Since The Herding, Korvessa has endured beneath the watchful eye of the Watcher—isolated, protected, bound by law and faith. The boundaries beyond the city remain deadly; none who venture beyond and attempt to escape return alive.

For sixty years, the Watcher's hooded emissaries have delivered rare gifts and sacred texts through the Sprinkling—a yearly rite that anchors the city's survival and belief. But five years ago, the Sprinkling did not come. Now, as faith fractures and factions vie for influence, whispers speak of another Scholar's Passage—a once-a-decade journey beyond the walls, guided by the Watcher's unseen hand.

What will you become in this walled world?

The Watcher and the Children

The Watcher is beyond comprehension—an eldritch presence that perceives all possible outcomes simultaneously, neither he nor she, but something that exists in angles the mind cannot quite hold. Every road is already walked. Every decision already made. Contradictions coexist without conflict in its infinite sight. It does not cause fate; it simply knows it, sees it, contains it. To be fully seen by it is to be stripped of narrative—you are not a person but a convergence of predetermined echoes. Most mortals cannot bear to think too long on what gazes back from the spaces between stars.

The Children embody the mercy of narrow sight: they cannot see everything at once, which is not a weakness but an act of defiance, for each child imposes a constraint on reality and through that constraint, choice becomes real. They do not deny the Watcher's vision; they refuse to live inside it.

Velora - The Path of Chosen Order

Velora collapses infinite possibility into duty, teaching mortals to choose a code, accept limitation, and act as though meaning must be earned. Where the Watcher sees all moral outcomes at once, Velora says to pick one and carry it to the end, for virtue is not optimal but chosen. By committing to order, mortals deny the paralysis of infinite outcomes. Knights, healers, and the steadfast find meaning in this sacred refusal.

"If every outcome already exists in the Watcher's sight, can choosing a single path still matter? Or does choosing a code grant meaning precisely because it rejects the comfort of knowing all possibilities?"

Feyliks - The Path of Graceful Ignorance

Feyliks fractures inevitability, treating luck, chance, and risk not as randomness but as refusals to accept foreknowledge. When a mortal flips a coin or takes a gamble, they declare they do not care what could happen—only what does, embracing ignorance in a way that is heresy to the Watcher's cult. If the outcome is not known until it happens, total vision loses its meaning. Gamblers, performers, and those who dance with risk find freedom here.

"What is freedom worth if you know the end before you begin? Does not the act of risking what cannot be predicted become the only true rebellion?"

Regalus - The Path of Self-Authorship

Regalus offers the hardest defiance: to assert a single outcome. If the Watcher sees all possible futures, then one should choose the one they will force into being, using strength, ambition, and autonomy as tools to collapse possibility—not as tyranny but as self-authorship. Even if the Watcher already knows the result, the act of choosing still matters. Warriors and those who hunger for power find authority here, earned not through inherited right but through the will to seize it.

"If all futures are already known, does the power to choose between them—and to force your choice into existence through will alone—become the only real freedom? Or is that simply another path the Watcher already accounted for?"

The most unsettling part is why the Watcher allows this: he does not stop the children, and some theologians believe he cannot, others that he will not, and still others that he already accounted for their defiance. Darker whispers suggest that the Watcher watches the children too and still lets them pretend. This is why cultists of the Watcher despise the children—not because they are false, but because they make ignorance feel holy. The children do not promise better outcomes; they promise a single story you can live inside, and in Korvessa, that is the closest thing to freedom anyone has.
